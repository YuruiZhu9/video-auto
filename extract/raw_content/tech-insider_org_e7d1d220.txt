Microsoft dropped a bombshell on the AI industry on April 2, 2026, releasing three proprietary foundational models under its Microsoft AI (MAI) brand — signaling the clearest break yet from its $13 billion OpenAI partnership. The move, led by CEO of Microsoft AI Mustafa Suleyman, positions the $3.2 trillion tech giant as a direct competitor to OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic in the foundation model race, while raising urgent questions about the future of the most consequential corporate partnership in artificial intelligence history.

The three models — MAI-Transcribe-1, MAI-Voice-1, and MAI-Image-2 — represent the first major output from the MAI Superintelligence team formed in November 2025, and they arrive at a moment when Microsoft’s AI capital expenditures have reached a staggering $37.5 billion in a single quarter. With Azure AI revenue growing 39% year-over-year and a $25 billion AI revenue target for fiscal 2026, the stakes for Microsoft’s in-house AI models strategy could not be higher.

Microsoft Launches Three In-House AI Models: What Was Released
--------------------------------------------------------------

The April 2, 2026, release marks a watershed moment for Microsoft’s in-house AI ambitions. For the first time, the company is offering proprietary foundational models that directly compete with products from OpenAI, the very company Microsoft has invested more than $13 billion in since 2019. The three models target distinct segments of the multimodal AI stack: speech-to-text transcription, voice generation, and image creation.

**MAI-Transcribe-1** is Microsoft’s new speech-to-text model, and the company claims it is the most accurate transcription system currently available. The model supports 25 languages, processes audio 2.5 times faster than Microsoft’s own Azure Fast offering, and is specifically engineered to handle noisy real-world environments such as call centers, conference rooms, and open-plan offices. On the widely used FLEURS Word Error Rate (WER) benchmark, MAI-Transcribe-1 ranks first globally, outperforming OpenAI’s Whisper and Google’s Gemini audio capabilities. Microsoft is already testing integrations with Copilot and Teams, suggesting the model will become a core component of enterprise productivity workflows.

**MAI-Voice-1** handles the inverse task — generating natural-sounding speech from text. The model can produce 60 seconds of high-quality audio in just one second of processing time, and it enables the creation of custom voice profiles from short audio snippets. This positions it as a competitor to ElevenLabs, Amazon Polly, and Google’s WaveNet in the rapidly growing AI voice synthesis market, which Grand View Research estimates will reach $9.7 billion by 2028.

**MAI-Image-2**, which initially debuted on MAI Playground on March 19, 2026, generates both images and has video capabilities in development. The model ranks in the top three on the Arena.ai image generation leaderboard, offers 2x faster generation speeds compared to its predecessor with no quality degradation, and is priced at $5 per million text input tokens and $33 per million image output tokens. It is rolling out across Bing, PowerPoint, and Copilot, making it accessible to hundreds of millions of Microsoft users. All three models are available through the Microsoft Foundry developer platform and MAI Playground.

Inside the MAI Superintelligence Team: Mustafa Suleyman’s Mission
-----------------------------------------------------------------

The organizational architecture behind these models is as significant as the technology itself. The MAI Superintelligence team was formally established in November 2025 under the leadership of Mustafa Suleyman, the DeepMind co-founder who joined Microsoft as CEO of Microsoft AI in March 2024. Following a corporate reorganization announced by Satya Nadella in March 2026, Suleyman shifted away from day-to-day Copilot oversight to focus exclusively on frontier model development and superintelligence research — a move that underscores the strategic priority Microsoft places on building its own foundational AI capabilities.

The team has been rapidly assembling top-tier AI talent. Microsoft hired Ali Farhadi, former CEO of the Allen Institute for AI (AI2), along with several other researchers from the Seattle-based nonprofit. Suleyman’s team operates with what Microsoft describes as a “Humanist AI” philosophy — putting humans at the center, optimizing for practical communication, and training models for real-world utility rather than benchmark performance alone.

“We’re building something fundamentally different,” Suleyman said in a statement accompanying the release. “These models are lightning fast, they’re cheaper than anything from Google or OpenAI, and they’re designed for how people actually work.” The statement reflects Microsoft’s positioning strategy: competing not on raw intelligence (where GPT-5.4 and Claude Opus 4.6 still lead on general-purpose benchmarks) but on speed, cost-efficiency, and enterprise integration.

Microsoft began assembling the compute infrastructure for its frontier ambitions in October 2025, deploying a cluster of Nvidia GB200 chips. Suleyman has publicly stated that the company is “ramping over the next 12 to 18 months to get to frontier-scale compute,” with a goal of achieving state-of-the-art models across text, image, and audio generation by 2027. This timeline suggests that the current MAI releases are just the opening salvo in a much larger campaign.

The Renegotiated OpenAI Deal: How Microsoft Freed Itself
--------------------------------------------------------

The most significant backstory to Microsoft’s in-house AI models is the renegotiated partnership with OpenAI. Under the original terms established in 2019 and extended through 2023, Microsoft was **contractually prohibited from developing its own broadly capable AI models**. In exchange for exclusive cloud hosting rights and the license to embed ChatGPT across its product suite, Microsoft agreed to cede the foundational model layer to OpenAI.

That restriction disappeared in 2025 as part of a comprehensive renegotiation that reshaped the partnership. The new terms, which coincided with [OpenAI’s historic $110 billion funding round](/openai-110-billion-funding-round-2026/), gave Microsoft greater independence to build proprietary models while maintaining its role as OpenAI’s primary cloud provider. OpenAI still represents an estimated 45% of Microsoft’s cloud backlog, making the relationship deeply intertwined even as both parties diversify.

Dan Ives, Managing Director at Wedbush Securities, described the dynamic as “the most complex corporate partnership in technology history. Microsoft needs OpenAI for Azure revenue today, but they’re simultaneously building the capability to compete with OpenAI tomorrow. It’s a tightrope walk that only a company with Microsoft’s resources can attempt.”

The renegotiation also coincided with the broader Stargate project — a $500 billion AI infrastructure initiative involving OpenAI, SoftBank, and Oracle — which further complicates the competitive dynamics. Microsoft’s decision to launch MAI models while Stargate is still in its early phases suggests the company is hedging its bets on multiple fronts.

Microsoft’s $150 Billion AI Spending Spree: The Numbers Behind the Strategy
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

Microsoft’s in-house model development is backed by the most aggressive capital expenditure program in corporate history. The company’s AI-related spending has accelerated dramatically, with capital expenditures reaching $37.5 billion in Q2 FY26 (the quarter ending December 31, 2025) alone — roughly two-thirds of which went to GPUs and CPUs to meet customer demand that consistently exceeds supply. For the full fiscal year 2026, analysts project total capex between $97.7 billion (S&P estimate) and $120 billion or more (bullish projections).

The financial engine driving this spending is Azure, which reported cloud revenue of $51.5 billion in Q2 FY26, representing 39% year-over-year growth. AI services were a significant contributor, with earlier quarters showing that AI alone accounted for 16 percentage points of Azure’s growth rate. Microsoft has set a target of $25 billion in AI-related revenue by the end of fiscal 2026, encompassing Azure AI services, Copilot subscriptions, and the newly launched MAI model APIs.

Competitive Landscape: How MAI Models Stack Up Against Rivals
-------------------------------------------------------------

Microsoft’s in-house AI models enter a fiercely competitive market where OpenAI, Google, Anthropic, and Amazon are all vying for enterprise AI dominance. The MAI models’ positioning is notable for what they are — and what they are not. Unlike [GPT-5.4, Claude Opus 4.6, or Gemini 3.1](/chatgpt-vs-claude-vs-deepseek-vs-gemini-2026/), the current MAI lineup does not include a general-purpose large language model. Microsoft has acknowledged that its current models are “built for efficiency and trained on fewer data points than general-purpose workhorses.” The frontier LLM, according to Suleyman’s timeline, is 12 to 18 months away.

However, in their respective niches, the MAI models are genuinely competitive. MAI-Transcribe-1’s FLEURS benchmark leadership puts it ahead of both Whisper and Gemini for multilingual transcription. MAI-Voice-1’s 60-seconds-in-one-second generation speed represents a significant throughput advantage over most competitors. And MAI-Image-2’s pricing — $5 per million input tokens — undercuts both DALL-E 3 and Google’s Imagen 3 on a per-generation basis.

I’m not completely sold on the company’s economics and pricing of AI at scale, which revolves around add-on user licenses to access Copilot features. The MAI models need to prove they can drive incremental revenue, not just shift existing Azure workloads.”

The Microsoft Foundry Platform: Building an AI Developer Ecosystem
------------------------------------------------------------------

Central to Microsoft’s in-house AI models strategy is Microsoft Foundry, the commercial platform through which developers can access and deploy MAI models. Foundry serves as Microsoft’s answer to OpenAI’s API platform, Google’s Vertex AI, and Amazon’s Bedrock — a unified interface for model access, fine-tuning, and deployment. The platform launched alongside the MAI Playground, a large language model testing environment that became operational on March 19, 2026, the same day MAI-Image-2 first became publicly available.

The Foundry strategy is significant because it gives Microsoft control over the full AI value chain — from model training to deployment to monetization — without depending on OpenAI’s API layer. Developers building on Foundry can access MAI models with the same tooling, SDKs, and enterprise compliance features they already use for Azure services. This integration advantage is a key differentiator: a developer already using Azure for compute can add MAI models to their stack without onboarding to a new platform or managing separate billing relationships.

The pricing structure reflects Microsoft’s aggressive market positioning. MAI-Transcribe-1 offers what Microsoft describes as the best price-performance ratio in the transcription market, while MAI-Image-2’s token-based pricing is designed to undercut competitors on a per-generation basis. This mirrors Microsoft’s broader cloud strategy of competing on price and integration rather than on raw technological superiority — the same playbook that made Azure the world’s second-largest cloud platform.

Wall Street’s Divided Reaction: Bull Case vs. Bear Case
-------------------------------------------------------

The market’s response to Microsoft’s in-house AI pivot has been deeply divided. Bull analysts point to Azure’s 39% growth rate, the $80 billion backlog, and the competitive necessity of owning the model layer. Bear analysts focus on the eye-watering capex figures, the risk of alienating OpenAI, and the uncertain timeline for frontier model delivery.

Dan Ives of Wedbush Securities has maintained an outperform rating, calling the Q2 FY26 earnings period a “prove it moment” for Nadella while noting that “doubters are building” in the stock. His thesis rests on the premise that Microsoft’s distribution advantage — 1.5 billion Office users, 300 million Teams users — creates an unmatched surface area for AI monetization that no pure-play AI company can replicate.

On the bearish side, Ewan McIntyre, VP Analyst at Gartner, offered a broader industry critique: “What we’re seeing is emblematic of the Intelligence Supercycle. Big Tech companies are spending $650 billion on AI infrastructure. But the real challenge isn’t adoption — it’s meaningful value creation. Microsoft’s MAI models are impressive, but the question is whether enterprises will pay a premium for Microsoft-branded AI when they already have access to OpenAI through the same Azure platform.”

The 12% stock decline in January 2026 illustrates the market’s anxiety. Despite reporting 17% total revenue growth and 39% Azure acceleration, investors punished Microsoft for capex that appeared to outpace near-term revenue potential. The MAI model releases are Microsoft’s answer to that skepticism — an attempt to demonstrate that the billions being spent on compute infrastructure are producing tangible, monetizable products, not just hosting OpenAI’s workloads.

What This Means for the OpenAI Partnership
------------------------------------------

The most immediate question raised by Microsoft’s in-house AI models is their impact on the OpenAI relationship. The two companies remain deeply intertwined: OpenAI represents 45% of Microsoft’s cloud backlog, Microsoft provides the vast majority of OpenAI’s compute infrastructure, and GPT-5.4 remains the primary LLM powering Copilot and other Microsoft AI features. The renegotiated 2025 deal ensures both parties can pursue independent strategies, but the competitive tension is undeniable.

For OpenAI, Microsoft’s pivot introduces a new competitive dynamic. Previously, Microsoft’s investment essentially guaranteed a massive enterprise distribution channel for OpenAI’s models. Now, every MAI model that gains traction potentially displaces an OpenAI API call. In the transcription market, MAI-Transcribe-1 directly competes with Whisper. In image generation, MAI-Image-2 competes with DALL-E. The voice synthesis market is next. And when Microsoft’s frontier LLM arrives in 2027, the competition will extend to the core of OpenAI’s business.

This dynamic mirrors historical patterns in Big Tech. Amazon built its own advertising technology after partnering with Google. Apple developed its own silicon after partnering with Intel. Google built its own cloud infrastructure after partnering with AWS. In each case, the dependent partner eventually built competing capabilities once the relationship reached sufficient scale. Microsoft’s MAI strategy follows the same playbook, with the added complexity of a $13 billion investment that gives it both a financial interest in OpenAI’s success and a strategic interest in replacing OpenAI’s technology.

Enterprise Impact: Copilot, Teams, and Microsoft 365 Integration
----------------------------------------------------------------

The enterprise implications of Microsoft’s in-house AI models extend far beyond API pricing comparisons. Microsoft is already testing MAI-Transcribe-1 integration with both [Microsoft Teams](/slack-vs-microsoft-teams-2026/) and Copilot, suggesting that the model will power real-time meeting transcription, searchable conversation archives, and AI-generated meeting summaries across the Microsoft 365 ecosystem. For the 300 million monthly active Teams users, this means transcription quality improvements that arrive automatically through platform updates rather than requiring separate AI service subscriptions.

Microsoft has also announced planned July 2026 price increases for Microsoft 365 that will bundle more AI features, including Copilot Chat capabilities, into standard subscriptions. This bundling strategy effectively subsidizes MAI model deployment through the existing M365 revenue base, creating a distribution moat that standalone AI companies cannot easily replicate. An enterprise customer paying for Microsoft 365 E5 will get access to MAI-powered transcription, voice synthesis, and image generation without incremental per-use costs — a pricing structure that could dramatically accelerate adoption.

The competitive threat to OpenAI’s enterprise business is clear. If Microsoft can deliver “good enough” AI capabilities bundled into existing subscriptions, enterprises have less incentive to pay premium prices for standalone ChatGPT Enterprise or OpenAI API access. This is the classic platform bundling playbook that Microsoft has executed successfully throughout its history — from Internet Explorer to Teams to Azure itself.

The Road to a Frontier LLM: Microsoft’s 2027 Timeline
-----------------------------------------------------

While the current MAI models focus on specialized multimodal tasks, Microsoft’s stated ambition is to develop a frontier-class general-purpose large language model by 2027. Suleyman has been explicit about this timeline, noting that the company started deploying Nvidia GB200 chip clusters in October 2025 and is “ramping over the next 12 to 18 months to get to frontier-scale compute.” This trajectory would put Microsoft’s first frontier LLM in direct competition with GPT-6 (or whatever OpenAI’s next generation is called), Gemini 4, and Claude 5.

The technical foundation for this effort includes Microsoft’s existing research capabilities — the company has published foundational work on efficiency techniques, small language models (the Phi series), and retrieval-augmented generation. The Phi-4 family of models, while smaller than frontier systems, demonstrated that Microsoft’s research teams can produce models with competitive performance-per-parameter ratios. Scaling those techniques to frontier size is the explicit goal of the MAI Superintelligence team.

Satya Nadella addressed the timeline during the Q2 FY26 earnings call in January 2026: “The key to long-term competitiveness is shaping our infrastructure to support new high-scale workloads.” He noted that Microsoft added nearly 1 gigawatt of data center capacity during the quarter — enough to power approximately 750,000 homes — specifically to support AI training and inference workloads. This infrastructure buildout is the foundation on which the 2027 frontier LLM will be trained.

Historical Context: Microsoft’s Pattern of Strategic Vertical Integration
-------------------------------------------------------------------------

Microsoft’s move to build in-house AI models follows a decades-long pattern of strategic vertical integration. The company has repeatedly identified key technology layers controlled by partners, invested heavily in those partners for distribution and learning purposes, and then built competing capabilities once it understood the market dynamics. The Bing search engine (competing with Google after partnering on web search), Surface hardware (competing with OEMs after decades of Windows licensing), and Azure (competing with AWS after initially dismissing cloud computing) all followed this pattern.

The AI model play is arguably the highest-stakes version of this strategy Microsoft has ever attempted. The company invested $13 billion in OpenAI, gained exclusive cloud hosting rights and deep technology access, used that access to build Copilot into a revenue-generating platform, and is now developing competing models that could eventually replace OpenAI’s technology entirely. The difference from previous vertical integration moves is the scale — over $100 billion in annual capex — and the speed at which AI technology evolves, which compresses the timeline for building competitive parity.

The risk is equally unprecedented. If the MAI frontier LLM fails to reach competitive quality by 2027, Microsoft will have spent hundreds of billions on infrastructure while remaining dependent on OpenAI. If it succeeds but alienates OpenAI in the process, it could lose 45% of its cloud backlog. The company is essentially betting that its distribution advantages, enterprise relationships, and capital resources can compensate for arriving late to the frontier model race.

Five Predictions for Microsoft’s In-House AI Strategy
-----------------------------------------------------

Based on the current trajectory, market dynamics, and competitive pressures, here are five predictions for how Microsoft’s in-house AI models strategy will evolve over the next 12 to 24 months:

**1. MAI models will power the majority of Copilot features by late 2027.** Microsoft will systematically replace OpenAI model calls with MAI equivalents wherever the in-house models meet quality thresholds. Transcription, voice, and image generation will switch first, followed by coding assistance and document generation as the frontier LLM matures. This will reduce Microsoft’s per-query costs and eliminate its dependency on OpenAI’s API pricing and availability.

**2. The OpenAI partnership will evolve into a competitive coexistence by mid-2027.** Both companies will maintain their financial relationship — Microsoft’s equity stake and cloud hosting agreement — while competing directly in the enterprise AI market. This mirrors the Apple-Samsung relationship, where Samsung manufactures components for iPhones while competing with Apple in the smartphone market.

**3. Microsoft will acquire at least one AI research lab to accelerate its frontier model timeline.** The hiring of Ali Farhadi from AI2 signals Microsoft’s aggressive talent strategy. A full acquisition of a mid-size AI lab — potentially Cohere, Mistral, or a similar company — would provide both talent and pre-trained model architectures that could shave months off the 2027 timeline.

**4. Azure AI market share will surpass AWS Bedrock by Q4 2026.** The combination of OpenAI models, MAI models, and third-party models on a single platform — integrated with the broader Microsoft 365 ecosystem — gives Azure an unmatched breadth advantage. Enterprises that have already standardized on Microsoft’s productivity stack will find it increasingly difficult to justify a separate AI vendor relationship.

**5. Microsoft’s AI revenue will exceed $30 billion in fiscal 2027.** The current $25 billion target for FY26 is likely conservative given Azure’s growth trajectory. As MAI models reduce per-query costs while M365 price increases expand the revenue base, Microsoft could see AI become its fastest-growing revenue segment, potentially surpassing gaming and LinkedIn within two fiscal years.

Industry Implications: What Microsoft’s Move Means for the AI Market
--------------------------------------------------------------------

Microsoft’s decision to build in-house AI models sends shockwaves through an industry that has been structured around a clear division of labor: foundation model companies (OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind) build the models, and cloud platforms (Azure, AWS, GCP) host and distribute them. By collapsing these layers, Microsoft is following Google’s lead — where Gemini is both a product and a cloud service — and pressuring Amazon, which has struggled to build competitive foundation models despite its Nova and Titan offerings.

For enterprise customers, the shift creates both opportunities and risks. On the positive side, increased competition should drive down per-token pricing across all platforms. Microsoft’s decision to price MAI-Image-2 at $5 per million input tokens puts downward pressure on DALL-E and Imagen pricing. On the negative side, enterprises building on OpenAI’s API through Azure now face the risk of technology fragmentation — will Microsoft eventually deprecate OpenAI model access in favor of MAI equivalents?

For startups and smaller AI companies, the consolidation trend is alarming. When a $3.2 trillion company with 1.5 billion existing users decides to build its own transcription, voice, and image models, the addressable market for standalone AI API companies shrinks dramatically. Companies like ElevenLabs, Stability AI, and AssemblyAI face an existential question: can they maintain pricing power against a competitor that can bundle AI capabilities into a $36/month Microsoft 365 subscription?

The Talent War: How Microsoft Is Recruiting AI’s Best Researchers
-----------------------------------------------------------------

Behind the product announcements lies a fierce talent competition. Microsoft’s hiring of Ali Farhadi from the Allen Institute for AI — one of the most respected AI research organizations in the world — is just the most visible example of a broader recruitment campaign. The MAI Superintelligence team is drawing researchers from Google DeepMind, Meta FAIR, and academic institutions with compensation packages that reportedly include guaranteed equity grants worth $2 million or more over four years.

This talent acquisition strategy mirrors what Google did when building DeepMind and what Meta did when establishing FAIR — assembling a critical mass of top-tier researchers who can attract additional talent through their reputation and network effects. Suleyman himself is the most significant hire, bringing not only his DeepMind experience but also his vision for “Humanist AI” that prioritizes practical utility over benchmark optimization. His track record at DeepMind — where he led applied AI initiatives and co-founded the organization — gives the MAI team both credibility and a clear research agenda.

The talent war has implications beyond Microsoft. Every researcher who joins the MAI Superintelligence team is one fewer researcher available to OpenAI, Google, or Anthropic. In a market where the supply of frontier AI researchers is estimated at fewer than 5,000 individuals globally, Microsoft’s aggressive hiring creates real competitive headwinds for smaller companies that cannot match its compensation or infrastructure resources.

Risk Factors: What Could Derail Microsoft’s AI Model Strategy
-------------------------------------------------------------

Despite the scale of Microsoft’s commitment, several risk factors could undermine the strategy. The most significant is **execution risk on the frontier LLM**. Training a state-of-the-art language model from scratch is extraordinarily difficult — OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic have each spent years and billions of dollars developing their training pipelines, data curation processes, and alignment techniques. Microsoft’s research team, while talented, has never trained a model at frontier scale.

The second risk is **OpenAI retaliation**. If OpenAI perceives Microsoft’s MAI models as a competitive threat, it could seek to reduce its Azure dependency, diversify to other cloud providers (as it has already begun doing with Oracle and the Stargate project), or renegotiate terms that are less favorable to Microsoft. The loss of 45% of Azure’s cloud backlog would be a significant financial blow.

Third, there is **regulatory risk**. The European Union’s AI Act, which enters full enforcement in 2026, imposes significant compliance requirements on foundation model providers. Building and deploying frontier AI models requires navigating a complex regulatory landscape that OpenAI, Google, and Anthropic have already invested heavily in understanding. Microsoft’s in-house models will face the same scrutiny without the benefit of that institutional knowledge.

Finally, there is the **capital allocation risk**. With projected FY26 capex potentially exceeding $120 billion, Microsoft is making an enormous bet that AI revenue will justify the investment. If the AI revenue ramp proves slower than expected — or if a technological disruption (such as a breakthrough in efficient training techniques) makes large-scale compute less valuable — Microsoft could face significant write-downs and investor backlash.

Frequently Asked Questions
--------------------------

### What are Microsoft’s in-house AI models?

Microsoft’s in-house AI models, released under the MAI (Microsoft AI) brand on April 2, 2026, include MAI-Transcribe-1 (speech-to-text supporting 25 languages), MAI-Voice-1 (voice generation capable of producing 60 seconds of audio in one second), and MAI-Image-2 (image generation ranked in the top three on Arena.ai). These are the first proprietary foundational models developed by Microsoft’s MAI Superintelligence team, led by Mustafa Suleyman.

### Does this mean Microsoft is replacing OpenAI?

Not immediately. Microsoft maintains its $13 billion investment in OpenAI and continues to use GPT-5.4 as the primary LLM powering Copilot. However, the MAI models represent the beginning of a diversification strategy. Microsoft plans to develop a frontier-class general-purpose LLM by 2027, which would directly compete with OpenAI’s models. The renegotiated 2025 partnership deal removed the contractual restriction that previously prevented Microsoft from building its own broadly capable models.

### How do MAI models compare to OpenAI and Google alternatives?

In their specialized domains, MAI models are highly competitive. MAI-Transcribe-1 ranks first on the FLEURS benchmark, surpassing OpenAI’s Whisper and Google’s Gemini audio. MAI-Image-2 is in the top three on Arena.ai with pricing that undercuts DALL-E 3 and Imagen 3. However, Microsoft does not yet have a frontier general-purpose LLM to compete with GPT-5.4, Claude Opus 4.6, or Gemini 3.1.

### Where can developers access Microsoft’s MAI models?

All three MAI models are available through the Microsoft Foundry developer platform and MAI Playground. MAI-Image-2 is priced at $5 per million text input tokens and $33 per million image output tokens. The models integrate with existing Azure tooling, SDKs, and enterprise compliance features, making them accessible to developers already building on Microsoft’s cloud platform.

### How much is Microsoft spending on AI development?

Microsoft’s capital expenditures reached $37.5 billion in Q2 FY26 (ending December 2025), with approximately two-thirds going to GPU and CPU procurement. Full-year FY26 capex is projected at $97.7 billion to $120+ billion. The company targets $25 billion in AI-related revenue by the end of fiscal 2026, with Azure AI growing 39% year-over-year.

### Who leads Microsoft’s in-house AI development?

Mustafa Suleyman, co-founder of DeepMind and CEO of Microsoft AI, leads the MAI Superintelligence team formed in November 2025. Following a March 2026 reorganization, Suleyman shifted from Copilot oversight to focus exclusively on frontier model development. Key hires include Ali Farhadi, former CEO of the Allen Institute for AI.

### When will Microsoft release a frontier large language model?

According to Mustafa Suleyman, Microsoft is targeting state-of-the-art models across text, image, and audio generation by 2027. The company began deploying Nvidia GB200 chip clusters in October 2025 and is “ramping over the next 12 to 18 months to get to frontier-scale compute.” The current specialized MAI models are the first step toward this broader ambition.
